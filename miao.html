A minority of China

Miao

Section by Lilo Ryan

Introduction

The Miao group is found in Southwestern China, where the mountains cover most of the territory the minority group occupies. They live primarily in the Guizhou, Yunnan, and Hunan provinces. They are in the top five largest minority groups in China. Batik dyeing techniques and intricate embroidery make up the primary methods of artistic expression in Miao communities.

Objects

Existing piece

Miao Baby Carrier

Description

The Miao Baby Carrier has intricate and symmetric embroidery on the entire front, drawing attention to all of the intricate detailing. The butterfly sits at the bottom of the baby carrier with its wings spanning almost the entire width-wise distance. Additional motifs surround the central circular object.

Analysis

This Miao baby carrier has detailed imagery of the butterfly on it, connecting to Miao spiritual beliefs that all Miao people came from the original “Butterfly Mother”. In having the butterfly motif close to the baby and as part of a support to the baby, it further reinforces the idea that the spirituality of the people is held close to them. This piece holds both spiritual and functional purposes— it reflects the beliefs of the people who embroidered the original design and also the functional use of it as a way to hold a baby while continuing daily tasks and activities.

New piece

Miao Batik Jacket

Description

The variety of the different designs and patterns on this jacket give it a life and character of its own, one that is cohesive and symmetric in its overall design. The Miao jacket exemplifies fine craftsmanship and batik dyeing techniques embedded in the art form. It has detailed diamond patterns with cuffs that almost look like plaid. The shirt has a collar, merging European tailoring sensibilities with the artisanry of the minority groups. The indigo dye is likely from the bluegrass plant, used commonly for this purpose in Southwestern China.

Analysis

There is a clear functional purpose of the art— it is meant to be worn, even if the designs on it are incredibly complex. One paper that addresses the variety of Miao batik, which is greatly tied to the different images that are possible to represent, describes how different regions and groups can be clearly identified by the batik art they produce (Lyu and Yahaya 13). In this piece, the integration of batik patterns on what appears to be formal wear enhances the cultural purpose of the art as a functional and tangible element of cultural history and symbolism.
