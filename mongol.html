A minority of China

Mongol

Section by Zhirui Lin

Introduction

The Mongols are concentrated in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region, though substantial communities also live in Liaoning, Jilin, Heilongjiang, Xinjiang, and Qinghai. According to China’s 2020 national census, the Mongol population in China is around 6.3 million, making them one of the country’s officially recognized ethnic minorities. Most Mongols today practice Tibetan Buddhism, a tradition introduced through 16th-century contact with Tibetan lamas, but shamanic practices predating Buddhism are still observed in some communities. Felt yurts (ger), embroidered deel robes, and silver-and-coral ornaments such as the Ordos headdress below give Mongol material culture its distinct visual signature.

Objects

New piece

Headdress Adorned with Coral Beadwork and Turquoise

By Zhirui Lin

Description

This Headdress Adorned with Coral Beadwork and Turquoise dates to the first half of the 20th century and comes from Ordos in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. It is 10 cm wide and made of silver inlaid with coral and turquoise. The piece belongs to the Ordos type of Mongolian women’s headgear, characterized by its large size, heavy weight, sophisticated decoration, and exquisite workmanship (Shanghai Museum). It is currently displayed in the Chinese Minority Nationalities’ Art Gallery of the Shanghai Museum.

Analysis

In Mongolian costume, women’s headgear varies the most across regional and tribal clans, and a single set of headgear has historically been worth “many horses or hundreds of camels” (Shanghai Museum). Among Mongolian tribes, the Ordos head-ornaments are “the most complicated and expensive” and function as a symbol of wealth and social status (Shanghai Museum). The materials themselves carry meaning: in comparable Chahar Mongol headdresses held by the Rubin Museum of Himalayan Art, similar coral-and-silver constructions feature auspicious imagery such as silver bats holding peaches that translates as the Chinese phrase “may you possess both blessings and longevity” (福壽雙全), suggesting that these headdresses are not only displays of wealth but also vehicles for blessings on the wearer (Rubin Museum). Functionally, the headdress is tied to a married woman’s public identity: in Ordos custom, women wear such headgear when they appear in public. By concentrating coral, turquoise, and silver into a single form weighing several kilograms, the Ordos headdress materializes the family’s status and wealth on the body.
