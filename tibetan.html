A minority of China

Tibetan

Section by Zhirui Lin and Dominic Jackman

Introduction

The Tibetan people primarily inhabit the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, with substantial communities also residing in the Tibet Autonomous Region as well as in the provinces of Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu, and Yunnan. According to the Britannica, the population of this region consists “almost entirely of Tibetans,” alongside Han, Hui, Monpa, Lhoba, and other ethnic minorities. The majority of Tibetans practice Tibetan Buddhism, while a minority continue to follow Bon, an indigenous shamanistic tradition that predates Buddhism. Tibetan visual and material culture is inextricably linked to monastic and ritual life, integrating functions of religious devotion with exquisite craftsmanship.

Objects

Existing piece

Tibetan Buddhist Woodblock

By Zhirui Lin

Description

This is a double-sided Tibetan woodblock used for printing, measuring 5½ × 19½ in. Everything on the block is carved in reverse: it was never meant to be looked at directly, but inked and pressed onto paper to produce repeated images and texts (Lin and Jackman). The front features five symmetrical panels: three Buddhas in distinct mudras (vitarka, dhyana, and dharmachakra) flanked by two stupas, separated by floral and cloud motifs, with reversed Tibetan script in the formal uchen style running along the bottom edge (Lin and Jackman).

Analysis

The reverse side of the block carries six syllables in bold red, Om Mani Padme Hum, which are the most sacred mantra in Tibetan Buddhism, written in a Sanskrit-embellished Tibetan script (Lin and Jackman). Each syllable corresponds to one of the six perfections: generosity, ethics, patience, diligence, concentration, and wisdom (Dalai Lama). Functionally, woodblock printing was adopted in Tibet starting from the 12th century, and a piece like this likely comes from one of the major printing houses such as Derge or Nartang during the 15th to 19th centuries (Trinlé and Gonkatsang). Generally, woodblock printing was commissioned by monasteries or wealthy sponsors to earn spiritual merit and required a complex division of labor among engravers, scribes, and proofreaders (Trinlé and Gonkatsang). Its durability meant that a single block could produce thousands of sacred prints over centuries, each carrying a blessing to its recipient (Lin and Jackman). The mantra on the back transforms this woodblock from a functional object into a sacred one, and invites us to consider what it means when a tool also becomes a prayer (Lin and Jackman).

Existing piece

Tibetan Amulet Box (Gau)

By Dominic Jackman

Description

The Tibetan amulet box, also known as a gau, is, as the name suggests, a box that contains amulets to shield the owner from some malady or misfortune. It would often be worn or carried close to the body. The box itself is highly detailed, with gold ornamentation on the ridges, and golden deities surrounding the empty center.

Analysis

The gau is important because it demonstrates how the Tibetan Buddhist faith was carried into one’s daily life. It combined decorative and spiritual function, acting both as a symbol of faith and as protection through Buddhism. The detailed figures and symbols on the surface add to this protective meaning, making the box feel like more than just a container. Its empty center also suggests that the most sacred part was once inside, showing how the object’s meaning came from both its appearance and its hidden contents.

New piece

Kapala (Skull Bowl)

By Dominic Jackman

Description

Kapala: sacred skull bowl. The one pictured has three parts: a copper bowl cover, a skull bowl, and a copper bowl rest. It is an important receptacle for Dharma, a key concept of Eastern Buddhism. The bowl itself is made from a skull, as well as bronze parts.

Analysis

The Kapala is an important work of art, turning the human skull, a symbol of death, into a sacred vessel with ritual properties. Functionally, the kapala is used as a ritual offering vessel in Tibetan Buddhist and Vajrayana practices. It can hold symbolic offerings such as wine, nectar, or ritual cakes, which are presented to deities during ceremonies. The Global Nepali Museum describes the kapala as an altar, consecration, and sacrificial object, while Project Himalayan Art explains that skull cups often appear in the hands of tantric Buddhist masters and deities. This shows that the kapala is not only decorative, but an active ritual object used in worship and meditation.

The Met notes that the skull cup can symbolize detachment, while the Asian Art Museum explains that skull bowls remind devotees of impermanence and the need to work toward salvation. This makes the kapala a powerful example of religious art that is meant to confront death rather than hide from it. These various interpretations of the kapala show that one object can carry various meanings at once, showing the breadth and layers of tibetan art.
